## 1. The premise

People lie to you for free, and they do it to be nice. "The Mom Test" (Rob Fitzpatrick) is the rule that keeps that from contaminating your research: **ask questions even your mom couldn't lie to you about.** That means questions about *what someone has already done* and *what it cost them*, never questions that invite a polite opinion about your idea.

Your job in an interview is not to pitch. The moment you describe your idea, the person switches from reporting their life to protecting your feelings. Hold the idea back until the very end, if at all.

## 2. Question patterns that work

Start broad and let them narrow. Sequence matters more than any single question.

- **Context:** "Tell me about the last time you had to <do the thing>." (Ask for a *specific instance*, not a general practice.)
- **Recurrence:** "How often does that come up? When was the last time before that?"
- **Cost:** "What does that cost you — in money, or in time?" Get a number.
- **Current behaviour:** "What do you do about it today? Walk me through it step by step."
- **Alternatives:** "Have you looked for a tool for this? What did you find? Why didn't it stick?"
- **Consequences:** "What happens if you get this wrong / skip it?"
- **Commitment:** "What would have to be true for you to pay to make this go away?"

The gold is in specifics. Every time they say "usually" or "I always," ask "when was the last time you actually did that?"

## 3. Anti-patterns (what not to ask)

| Do not ask | Why it fails | Ask instead |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| "Would you use a tool that…?" | Invites a free, hypothetical yes | "What do you use today for this?" |
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | Asks for a favour, not a fact | "Tell me about the last time this was a problem." |
| "How much would you pay for X?" | Hypothetical money is imaginary money | "What do you pay for this today, even indirectly?" |
| "Wouldn't it be great if…?" | Leading question, telegraphs your answer | "What's the most annoying part of <task>?" |
| "Do you have this problem?" | Everyone says yes to be agreeable | "When did it last happen? What did you do?" |

## 4. Handling the three failure modes

- **The compliment** ("That sounds incredible!"). Do not thank them and move on. Deflect with: "Thanks — but I'm trying to learn, not sell. When did you last hit this problem?" Compliments are a signal that you pitched too early.
- **The fluff / hypothetical** ("I usually just…", "I'd probably…"). Anchor to reality: "When was the last time that actually happened? What did you do that day?"
- **The zombie lead** ("Let me think about it."). This is a no in disguise. End politely and record it as a *no* — do not follow up hoping it turns into a yes.

## 5. Eliciting commitment and numbers

Opinions are cheap; commitments are evidence. At the end of a good conversation, ask for something that costs them:

- A pre-order or deposit.
- An introduction to someone with the same problem.
- Permission to follow up with a specific next step ("can I show you a prototype next Tuesday?").

Currency of commitment, weakest to strongest: a compliment → an email address → a scheduled meeting → a letter of intent → a deposit → a paid pilot. Record *which level* each person reached. A short waitlist of emails is far weaker than three deposits.

## 6. Note-taking format for synthesis

Capture verbatim quotes, not your interpretation. Per interview, record:

```
Date:
Who (role, company size, ICP bucket):
Trigger event (the last time the problem occurred):
Current workaround:
Cost (money / time):
Verbatim quotes:
Commitment level reached (0–6):
Their words for the problem (their jargon, not yours):
```

Their **jargon** is the raw material for your positioning and landing-page copy — collect it deliberately.

## 7. When to stop

Stop when the last three interviews stopped surprising you and the same themes, workflows, and workarounds keep repeating. In one narrow segment that is usually 15–30 conversations. If every interview surprises you, you are talking to too broad a set of people — pick one ICP and go again.
