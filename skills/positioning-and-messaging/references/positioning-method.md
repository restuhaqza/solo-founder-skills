## 1. What positioning actually is

Positioning is not a tagline and not a slogan. It is the **context you deliberately
place around a product** so the right buyer understands its value without effort.
April Dunford's definition in *Obviously Awesome* is the useful one: positioning is
the set of choices — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit
customer, market category — that make a product's strengths obvious to the people
who will value them most.

Two consequences follow, and both change how you work:

- **Positioning is relative.** It only exists next to an alternative the buyer is
  already considering. No alternatives, no positioning.
- **Positioning is a choice, not a discovery.** You can place the same product in
  different categories and reach different buyers. The job is to pick the frame
  that serves the buyer you want.

The output is never a single sentence in isolation. It is a chain of agreements:
the category implies who plays here, the buyer implies what they value, the value
implies which attributes matter, and the attributes imply which alternatives you
beat.

## 2. The Dunford five components

Work these in order. Each depends on the one before it; skipping ahead produces a
headline that sounds fine and means nothing.

**1. Competitive alternatives.** What would the buyer do if your product did not
exist? List everything, not just direct competitors: spreadsheets, group chats,
manual processes, a general-purpose incumbent used badly, and "do nothing." This
is the baseline all value is measured against.

**2. Unique attributes.** What do you have that those alternatives do not? State
attributes as facts, not praise: "exports to the ledger", "runs on-device",
"flat pricing", "works with no account". Honesty matters — an attribute the buyer
cannot verify is marketing, not differentiation.

**3. Value.** For each attribute, ask "so what?" until you hit something the buyer
would repeat to a colleague. Attributes are features; value is the change in the
buyer's day. This translation is the whole game, and it is where most weak
products fail — they list attributes and call it value.

**4. Best-fit customer.** Which buyer cares about that value *most*? Not who
*could* use it — who feels the pain sharpest and would pay first. Characteristics
to narrow by: the industry, the role, the workflow, the scale, the stage of the
company. The narrower the segment, the stronger and more specific the messaging.

**5. Market category.** The frame that tells the buyer what this is. It sets
expectations, names the competition, and triggers a budget line the buyer already
has. Positioning *within* an existing category is usually far cheaper than
inventing one — see the next section.

## 3. Choosing a market category

The category is a lens. Choose the one that makes your strongest value obvious to
your best-fit customer. Ask three questions:

- **What does the buyer call this problem today?** If they search "invoice software"
  and you call yourself an "accounts-receivable workflow engine", you have made
  yourself hard to discover.
- **Which category makes your differentiating attribute feel essential rather than
  nice-to-have?** If your advantage is speed, a category where speed is table stakes
  hides it; a category where speed is the promise showcases it.
- **Where does the money already sit?** A familiar category reuses a budget line
  the buyer has approved before. An unfamiliar one asks them to create a new one.

Sketching two or three candidate categories against the same product is normal.
Write each candidate down with the buyer it implies and the value it foregrounds,
then pick the one where your attributes score highest.

## 4. Creating a new category (and what it costs)

Sometimes the existing categories genuinely misrepresent the product — every
available frame buries the value. Creating a new category is a real strategic move,
but it is expensive:

- **You must teach the market that the problem exists** before they can want your
  solution. That is education spend, and it is slow.
- **You lose the ready-made budget line.** The buyer now has to justify a new kind
  of purchase, to themselves and often to a manager.
- **You become the reference for the category**, which competitors will borrow —
  often with more distribution than you.
- **It only pays off when the new frame is obviously better** than any existing one,
  and when you can afford to keep explaining it for a long time.

The cheap default is to position within an existing category and differentiate
inside it. Reach for a new category only when no existing frame lets your best-fit
buyer see the value. When you do, say so explicitly in `positioning.md` and name
the cost you are accepting.

## 5. Worked before/after examples

These are illustrative rewrites, not claims about real products. Notice that the
*after* always names an alternative, a buyer, and a change — and never invents a
number.

**Example A — a scheduling tool used by studios**
- Before: *"The all-in-one, seamless platform for modern teams."*
- Why it fails: no alternative named, no buyer, "all-in-one" and "seamless" are
  filler, and nothing says what changes.
- After: *"Stop losing bookings to missed DMs — a scheduler for tattoo studios that
  lives where your clients already message you."*
- Why it works: names the alternative behaviour (missed DMs), the buyer (tattoo
  studios), and the change (fewer lost bookings), with the mechanism as proof.

**Example B — a reporting tool for solo consultants**
- Before: *"We're building the future of client reporting."*
- Why it fails: company-first, a category nobody owns, no buyer, no verb that
  promises anything.
- After: *"Turn a messy month of client work into a clean report in one sitting."*
- Why it works: outcome-led, states a before → after, and the "who" is carried by
  the context of the page. Add "for independent consultants" if the page must
  stand alone.

**Example C — a spreadsheet replacement**
- Before: *"A powerful, flexible spreadsheet alternative."*
- Why it fails: "powerful" and "flexible" are the incumbent's own words; this is a
  weaker version of the leader's frame.
- After: *"The shared finance sheet your team can't accidentally break — no formulas
  to memorize."*
- Why it works: names the alternative (a shared sheet), the real risk (breakage),
  and the value (no formula wrangling), and picks its own frame instead of copying.

## 6. A checklist before you call it done

- [ ] At least one alternative is a spreadsheet, a chat, or "do nothing".
- [ ] Every attribute is a verifiable fact, not an adjective.
- [ ] Each attribute has a "so what?" value attached.
- [ ] The best-fit customer is narrow enough to feel like one person.
- [ ] The category is one the buyer already uses, or the cost of a new one is
      written down and accepted.
- [ ] The one-liner names an alternative and a gap.
- [ ] No invented metrics, testimonials, or comparative claims — those are the
      founder's to supply and approve.
- [ ] A stranger can repeat the hero after five seconds (Step 9).
