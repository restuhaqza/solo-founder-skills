# Content Strategy

How a solo founder chooses what to write, holds a cadence alone, and gets more
out of each piece than the writing itself.

**Contents**
1. The pillar-and-cluster model
2. Cadence you can actually hold
3. Choosing topics from real customer language
4. Build-in-public specificity
5. The atomisation pattern

---

## 1. The pillar-and-cluster model

A content program is a small library, not a stream of unrelated posts. The unit is
a **cluster**: one **pillar** and three or four **supporting pieces** that hang off
it.

- **The pillar** is your single best, most complete answer to your buyer's biggest
  question — the guide they would bookmark and send to a colleague. It is long,
  specific, and opinionated. Its job is to be the canonical page for that question,
  so everything else can point at it.
- **The supporting pieces** are narrower. Each answers one sub-question the pillar
  raises: a comparison, a how-to for one step, a template, a teardown of a real
  problem, a cost breakdown. Each one **links up to the pillar**, and the pillar
  **links down** to each.

Why clusters beat a pile of posts: search engines and AI assistants read a cluster
as a topic you own, not isolated pages. Authority accrues to the group. A single
great post with five thin siblings that ignore it is a set of orphans competing with
each other; a pillar with five supporting pieces that cross-link is a small
authority you can build on.

Keep the cluster coherent. If a supporting piece does not obviously relate to the
pillar's question, it belongs in a different cluster — or in next month's.

## 2. Cadence you can actually hold

The failure mode is not too few posts; it is a burst followed by silence.
Compounding only works if you do not stop, so set the rhythm to what a single
founder can sustain for a **year**, not what looks impressive for a month.

A defensible default:

- **One pillar per month.**
- **Three to four supporting pieces per month**, each linked to the pillar.
- **One engineering-as-marketing artifact per quarter** (a free tool, a comparison
  page, a public dashboard) — a permanent asset rather than a post.

That is roughly one publishable thing a week. If that is too much at first, drop to
a pillar every six weeks and two supporting pieces — but keep the interval
*regular*. Consistency is the asset. A missed month is not a disaster; stopping is.

Time-box the writing. If the pillar is consuming three weeks, it is too broad —
narrow the question until it fits the time you have, because the supporting pieces
and distribution still have to happen.

## 3. Choosing topics from real customer language

The most common content mistake is writing what you want to say instead of what the
buyer is already trying to find. Mine the raw material you already have:

- **The first sales-call question.** The question that opens every conversation is
  the pillar. Write it down verbatim — that phrasing is your target query.
- **Support and onboarding email.** Questions people ask *after* signing up are
  supporting pieces: they are the sub-questions the pillar implies.
- **Search suggestions and "people also ask".** Type your topic into a search box
  and read the autocomplete and the related questions. This is free demand data:
  what people actually type, in their words.
- **Community threads.** The questions that get asked and re-asked in the places
  your buyer gathers are evergreen topics. The fact that it was asked again is the
  signal.
- **Your own changelog.** Bugs you fixed and experiments you ran are specific,
  hard-to-copy topics nobody else can write.

Prefer the buyer's phrasing over your own. "How much should I charge for a logo"
beats "Pricing methodology for design services" every time, because that is what
gets typed into the search box and spoken to an assistant.

## 4. Build-in-public specificity

Generic build-in-public is tired. "We shipped a feature 🚀" and "big things coming"
teach a reader nothing and earn no search demand. **Specificity** is the
differentiator:

- the pricing experiment you ran and what it did,
- the churn investigation and what you found,
- the exact bug a user hit and how you fixed it,
- the number that moved, with its real value,
- the mistake you made and what it cost.

Three specific posts a month beat thirty vague ones. These posts also make good
supporting pieces when they answer a buyer sub-question — a teardown of a real
problem is both build-in-public and a search asset. The test for a specific post:
could a reader repeat the insight to someone else tomorrow? If not, it is
filler.

Keep claims honest and self-approving: numbers should be yours, dated, and real.
If you are not ready to publish a number, write the post without it.

## 5. The atomisation pattern

The gain is in **atomisation, not creation**. One finished piece contains enough
material for a week of distribution. When a piece is done, run it through this
table and produce the slices *immediately*, while the material is fresh — do not
leave it for a day that never comes.

| Slice | What it becomes | Surface |
|-------|-----------------|---------|
| The whole piece | the pillar or supporting page | your site |
| Three most surprising points | a short thread | X / LinkedIn |
| A walk-through of one step | a short video script | YouTube / Shorts / TikTok |
| One section, tightened | a newsletter section | your list |
| The core question, asked | a genuine answer with no pitch | the community where the buyer gathers |
| A derived list or template | a downloadable/checklist | lead magnet, repo, gist |

Rules that keep atomisation from backfiring:

- **Rewrite for each surface; never cross-post identical text.** A thread is not a
  blog post chopped into tweets, and a video script is not a blog post read aloud.
  The same insight, shaped for the room.
- **Log the slices.** Keep a running "one thing → many pieces" note so a good piece
  is never published once and forgotten. A piece with no logged slices is
  unfinished.
- **Do not atomise junk.** Atomisation multiplies a piece; if the piece is thin,
  you have just made a lot of thinness. Fix the source first.
