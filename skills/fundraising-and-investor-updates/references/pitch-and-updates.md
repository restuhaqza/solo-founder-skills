## 1. The deck's job

A deck is not a document; it is a rehearsal of a conversation. Its job is to make an investor believe one specific thing about the future — the thing your company is positioned to capture — and then to hand them the numbers that make that belief checkable. Everything that does not serve those two goals is noise.

Practical consequences:

- **One idea per slide, one line per idea.** If a slide needs a paragraph of explanation, it is two slides or none.
- **Lead with the insight, not the product.** Anyone can describe what the product does. Your edge is the non-obvious thing you understand about the problem — the reason the obvious solution does not work and yours does.
- **Every number has a source.** A traction figure that cannot be traced to a real export is a liability the moment diligence starts. Keep the source next to the number in your working copy and cut it from the slide if it has none.
- **Design the ask.** The deck ends by telling the investor what you want (amount, instrument, milestone) and what happens next. A deck without a ask is a brochure.

Keep it short. Fifteen to twenty slides is plenty; a solo round needs a crisp, unfakeable story more than a long book.

## 2. Deck narrative, section by section

This is the spine — use `assets/pitch-deck-outline.md` to fill it in. The order matters because each slide earns the next.

1. **Problem.** Whose pain, how often, and what it costs them today. Use a specific person and a specific moment, not a market statistic.
2. **Insight.** The non-obvious truth your solution is built on: why the existing alternatives fail and what you understand that others do not. This is the slide that makes the rest credible.
3. **Product.** What it does, in one breath, tied back to the insight. Show, do not narrate; a screenshot or a 20-second demo beats a feature list.
4. **Traction.** The real numbers that prove people want this — see §3 for what to show when. Honest and specific beats large and vague.
5. **Market.** Who the customer is and how you reach them, sized from the bottom up (buyers × price) rather than a top-down percentage of a big industry number.
6. **Business model.** How you make money and why the unit economics work: price, cost to serve, and the path to a healthy margin.
7. **Go-to-market.** The specific channels that have produced customers so far, and the repeatable motion you will scale with the money. Name what you have actually done, not a channel wishlist.
8. **Team.** Why you, for this, now. For a solo founder that means relevant advantage (you lived the problem, you have the audience, you have shipped this before) plus the honesty that you know what you are missing.
9. **The ask.** The amount, the instrument, the milestone the money buys, and the runway it provides — the Step 2 sentence, stated plainly.
10. **Use of funds.** Where the money goes, split by category (hiring, product, GTM, infrastructure), tied to reaching the milestone. A round with no spending plan reads as a round without a plan.

## 3. What traction to show at each stage

Show the most advanced *credible* number you actually have; do not fake a later stage with a thin number. Investors pattern-match honesty fast.

- **Pre-seed (idea to first users).** The proof that the problem is real and you can find the people who have it: customer conversations with verbatim quotes, a waitlist or pilot pipeline, early usage from a handful of real users, any revenue at all. Founder-market fit is a legitimate traction slide here.
- **Seed (first paying customers).** The proof that some people will pay and stay: revenue and its growth, a customer count with names (or clear segments), a first retention signal, activation or usage that correlates with paying. Show the shape of the curve, not a single point.
- **Post-seed / Series A (repeatable motion).** The proof that the growth is a *machine*, not a spike: consistent month-over-month growth, healthy retention and unit economics (payback, gross margin), and at least one channel that reliably produces customers. Churn quality and revenue concentration matter as much as the top line.

For every stage, prefer *cohort and trend* over a headline. "Eleven customers, $1.4K MRR, growing for five months, 8 of 11 still active at month three" is far stronger than "growing fast." If you must show a projection, label it a projection and state the assumption.

## 4. The monthly investor update: template and cadence

The update is how a single check becomes a relationship, and it is the same shape every month. Use `assets/investor-update.md`. The sections:

- **Subject line** — `<Company> — <Month> update` and, if you are brave and honest, a one-phrase headline (including a hard month).
- **One-line summary** — where the business is and the single most important thing this month.
- **The numbers** — the same 3–5 metrics every month with last month's value next to this month's, so the trend is visible at a glance. Pull these from `product-metrics`; do not re-derive them here.
- **Highlights** — what went well, as outcomes not activity.
- **Lowlights / what's hard** — the miss, stated plainly and early. This is the section founders omit and investors most want.
- **What I'm doing about it** — the plan for the lowlight.
- **Asks** — exactly one clear, specific ask (an intro, a candidate, a customer, a piece of advice). One ask gets answered; five get skimmed.
- **Thanks** — a short, genuine close.

**Cadence:** the same day every month, without exception. Put it on the calendar like a customer commitment. Consistency is the signal; a founder who reports on the 3rd every month, including the bad months, is visibly more investable than one who reports when there is news. BCC the list or use a small mailing list, keep it short, and write it in your own voice — this is one of the few documents investors read in full.

## 5. The golden rule: update on bad news too

**The month you least want to send the update is the month it matters most.** Missing a down month, burying the miss under good news, or going silent until the recovery are all the same mistake: they convert a datapoint into a trust problem. Investors expect variance — early businesses are lumpy. What they cannot forgive is being surprised, because surprise is what makes them unable to help and unwilling to follow on.

So report the miss early, with the plan and the ask. A founder who writes "we lost our biggest customer, here is why, here is what I changed, here is the help I need" six weeks before the bad news would have surfaced is doing exactly the job an investor is paying for with their attention. That is the behavior that gets the next round funded — often by the same people, often before it was even announced.

## 6. The data room

The data room is the folder you hand over when diligence starts. Its contents are in `assets/data-room-checklist.md`; the sections are:

- **Company** — incorporation, good standing, cap table (current and fully diluted), any SAFEs/notes outstanding, board/consent records.
- **Financials** — revenue by month, P&L or a clean summary, bank statements, the payment-processor export, and any tax filings.
- **Product & metrics** — what the product does, the metric definitions, and the dashboards or exports behind the numbers you showed.
- **Customers & contracts** — the customer list (or segments), key agreements, and the concentration picture.
- **Team & IP** — founder agreements, contractor and IP assignment, any open-source or third-party dependencies that matter.
- **Legal & risk** — terms of service, privacy policy, insurance, and anything a lawyer has flagged.

Two rules: **index it** (one place, clear filenames, a short README) and **read what you put in it** — you are accountable for every document. Complete the essentials early and mark the rest "as asked," so diligence finds you organized rather than scrambling.
